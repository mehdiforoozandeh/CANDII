Motivationthe question

Can a model turn one track into another, from its processing settings alone?

  • CANDI reads these settings (we call them covariates) for every input and output track.
  • We test the settings in isolation, with small models, on data where the true X′ exists.
Backgroundthe data

We rebuilt 7 tracks many times, changing one setting at a time

Each rebuild is called an arm. We reran the ENCODE pipeline from the raw reads, so for every change we have the true answer X′. Results are always reported per mark class.

Backgroundthe model

A small network g reads the settings and builds the transformation f

  • We train g, not f. f is only what g outputs for one pair of settings.
  • f outputs a distribution in every bin (a mean and a spread), as CANDI does.
Backgroundfour designs of f

Four designs of f, from simple to flexible

A and B look at one bin at a time, so they change magnitude only. C and D also see neighbouring bins, so they can change shape (for example, peak width).

Backgroundrows and versions of g

Each design runs in two rows, and with two versions of g

  • Row 1: one f for the whole genome. Row 2: g also reads each bin's own level, so f can treat low bins and peaks differently. Row-2 designs carry a 2: A2, B2, C2, D2.
  • One g per track: 7 small g's. One g across tracks: a single g that also reads the assay.
Backgroundscoring

Two scores, one split, and one bar for "larger than noise"

Lower is better for every score. All scores are on held-out chromosomes (chr19 and chr21); the best training step is picked on chr22.

Motivationthe t118 grid

The t118 grid had two problems

  • The depth law: keep everything, change only the read depth, and the predicted counts should scale by the read ratio. We allow a 10% error.
  • A blow-up: a (pair, seed) record with CRPS above 20, where a typical value is below 1. One blow-up can dominate a mean.
Motivationwhy, and what we changed

Two causes, and one fix for each

Part 1 · methodthe depth offset

Part 1: f now works on counts divided by the read depth

  • The only input change from the old grid is a constant shift per track: − log d.
  • g still reads both depths, so g can move f away from d′/d. The offset sets the default; it does not force the depth law.

Part 1 · methodthe spread

Each design's spread now sees what its mean sees

  • The spread is the negative binomial's shape n: how wide the predicted distribution is in each bin.
  • Only row-1 A changed. It had one spread per pair; now it sets its spread by level, as A2 already did. Old-spread A keeps the old rule, so we can split the two changes.

Part 2 · methodthe map h

Part 2: compute −log10 p from the predicted counts, with a map h

part 2 running

Part 2 stops predicting p directly. f predicts a count distribution; h turns each possible count k, with the control, into a value P = −log10 p. The predicted P is the set h(k), each weighted by the predicted NB probability of k. No sampling is used.

Discussionwhat is still unclear

Four things to discuss

Should g read depth?
The offset sets d′/d as the default, but g reads both depths and moves away from it. Real D is among the furthest from it; its twin, which gets random settings, stays close to the law.
Why is A's gain in the spread?
Against old-spread A, A gains in CRPS but not in the MSE of the mean. Both changes cut A's scale error. Whether the offset moves A's mean we cannot say: the old grid has no MSE.
Why do B to D2 not move?
They could already bend and scale. A constant shift may add nothing they could not learn before.
A harder control
The twin now gets d′/d for free. What it still lacks is the rest of the settings, so the gap reads "beyond depth", not "settings at all".
