Motivationthe question

Can a model turn one track into another, from its processing settings alone?

  • CANDI reads these settings (we call them covariates) for every input and output track.
  • We test the settings in isolation, with small models, on data where the true X′ exists.

A track is a genome-wide signal, here in 25 bp bins. The same sample, processed with different settings (read depth, read length, control, and so on), gives different tracks. The question is whether the settings alone tell us how to go from one to the other.

The figure: on the left, a real H3K27ac track X; on the right, the same sample with fewer reads, X′. The model gets X and both sets of settings, C and C′, and must predict X′.

We need this because CANDI is conditioned on these settings, but inside CANDI we cannot see what the settings alone do.

Likely question: "Why small models?" So the settings are the only route to the answer; a big model could hide what the settings do.

Motivationthe data

We rebuilt 7 tracks many times, changing one setting at a time

Each rebuild is called an arm. We reran the ENCODE pipeline from the raw reads, so for every change we have the true answer X′. Results are always reported per mark class.

Panel (a): each dot is one knob we changed, with the level we moved it to. Green knobs change which reads enter the pileup, so they move both the counts and the p‑values. Purple knobs change only the control or the peak caller, so they move only the p‑values.

Panel (b): the 7 base tracks fall in three mark classes with very different shapes. DNase has no control, so it has fewer arms.

Each (source arm, target arm) of one track is a pair; the model learns to go from the source to the target.

Likely question: "Are the arms simulated?" No: every arm is a real rerun of the ENCODE pipeline from the raw reads.

Motivationthe model

A small network g reads the settings and builds the transformation f

  • We train g, not f. f is only what g outputs for one pair of settings.
  • f outputs a distribution in every bin (a mean and a spread), as CANDI does: a negative binomial over counts.

Top row: g is a small neural network (2 layers of 64). It reads the source settings C and the target settings C′ and outputs the parameters θ of f.

Bottom row: f is applied to the source track X, bin by bin, and outputs a distribution for each bin of X′: a mean (the line) and a spread (the band).

Because only g sees the settings, any skill f has at turning X into X′ must come through the settings.

Likely question: "Why a distribution, not one number?" Counts are noisy; CANDI also outputs a negative binomial, and the CRPS score needs a distribution.

Motivationfour designs of f

Four designs of f, from simple to flexible

A and B look at one bin at a time, so they change magnitude only. C and D also see neighbouring bins, so they can change shape (for example, peak width).

The letters A to D name four forms of f, used everywhere in the deck. A is one straight line in log space; B is a rising curve with 12 knots; C smooths with a kernel and then applies a curve; D is a small CNN whose layers g scales and shifts.

Read the panels left to right as "more flexible". The bottom line of each panel says what it can change and how many numbers g must output.

Likely question: "Why keep A if it is the weakest?" A is the easiest to read, and in t118 it showed the clearest problem; it is also where the offset helps most (result 2).

  • Row 1: one f for the whole genome. Row 2: g also reads each bin's own level, so f can treat low bins and peaks differently. Row-2 designs carry a 2: A2, B2, C2, D2.
  • One g per track: 7 small g's. One g across tracks: a single g that also reads the assay.

Panel (a), the rows: in row 1, g reads only the settings, so every bin gets the same f. In row 2, g also reads the bin's own level, so f can change from bin to bin (darker colour = higher level).

So one "design" in the deck is a letter (A to D) plus a row (no 2 = row 1, a 2 = row 2), and every design is trained with both versions of g.

Likely question: "Which version of g is better?" In t118, one g per track was better on DNase and the two tied on histone marks; this deck does not revisit that.

Motivationwhat went wrong in the earlier grid

The earlier grid (t118) had two problems

  • The depth law: change only the read depth, and the predicted counts should scale by the read ratio. We allow a 10% error.
  • A blow-up: a (pair, seed) record with CRPS above 20, where a typical value is below 1. One blow-up can dominate a mean.

Panel (a): each marker is one cell of the old grid (a design, a version of g and a mark class); the y-axis is how far the predicted total count is from the read ratio, on a log scale. The green band is within 10%. Almost every cell is far outside it.

Panel (b): how many (pair, seed) records blew up (CRPS above 20) when the old grid predicted −log10 p directly, summed over classes and both versions of g. Row 2 (orange) blew up in every design.

These are the two problems this work tries to fix: the counts ignore depth, and the p‑value predictions are unstable.

Likely question: "Is the depth law the right test?" It is the simplest change with a known answer: halve the reads and every bin halves on average.

Motivationwhy, and two ideas

Two causes, and one idea for each

Panel (a): real data. Fewer reads scale every bin by about the read ratio (the orange line, the base times the ratio, follows the filled arm). In the old grid, f copied the counts unless g learned to scale them, and it did not learn this well.

Panel (b): a formula, not data. The old grid predicted −log10 p with a log-normal whose spread σ was learned. The mean of a log-normal grows as e to the σ²/2, so a spread that drifts up makes the mean explode.

Idea 1 (part 1): build the read ratio into f as a fixed offset. Idea 2 (part 2): stop predicting p; predict counts, and compute p from them with a map h.

Likely question: "Why not just clip σ?" Clipping hides the drift; computing p from counts removes the learned spread from the p‑value path altogether.

Motivationthe two questions

This work asks two questions

Question 1 · the depth offset
If f scales the counts by the read ratio unless g says otherwise, do the predictions follow the depth law, and do they get closer to the truth?
Experiment 1: retrain the whole grid with the offset. Compare it with the earlier grid, and with a twin that cannot see the settings.
Question 2 · the map h
If f predicts only counts, how closely can a map h turn them into the −log10 p track, as h gets more inputs and more flexible? Does the chain f → h avoid the blow-ups?
Experiment 2: train h on the true counts and on f's predicted counts. Score it alone and in the chain f → h, against the earlier grid's p.

Exploratory: both questions get readings, with no threshold set in advance.

These are the two vault questions under the covariates question: the depth-offset question (q5) and the map-h question (q6). They are written so that the results section answers them in order: results 1 to 3 answer question 1, results 4 to 6 answer question 2.

Question 1 is about the counts only. Question 2 is about the p‑value track, first with perfect counts (how good can h be?) and then with f's predicted counts (how good is the whole chain?).

Both are exploratory by PI decision: the plan set no thresholds, so the deck reports readings, each with its wobble and bar.

Likely question: "Why both at once?" They share the count model: part 2 feeds the part-1 f into h.

Methodhow we score

How we score: CRPS and its two parts, the error of the mean, and a bar for noise

Lower is better for every score. All scores are on held-out chromosomes (chr19 and chr21); the best training step is picked on chr22. Each score is a mean over (pair, seed) records.

(a) CRPS scores the whole predicted distribution against the true value in each bin: a prediction that is off, or too wide, scores high. (b) We split every CRPS into an oracle-scaled part (what is left after the best single rescale) and a scale error (what that rescale removes). A gain in scale error means "the overall factor was wrong", which is cheap to fix.

(c) The MSE of the mean looks only at the predicted mean, on log(1 + value). With CRPS it tells a gain in the mean from a gain in the spread.

(d) Every model is trained with 3 seeds. The wobble is the largest gap between two seeds, and a gain counts only if it is larger than the bar, 2 × the larger wobble of the two models.

Likely question: "Why not a p‑value test?" The run is exploratory; the bar is a simple, stated rule for "larger than seed noise".

Methodidea 1 · the depth offset

Idea 1: f works on counts divided by the read depth, so "do nothing" means "scale by d′/d"

  • The only input change from the old grid is a constant shift per track: − log d.
  • g still reads both depths, so g can move f away from d′/d. The offset sets the default; it does not force the depth law.

Lane (a), the old grid: f read log(1 + X) and output the log mean of X′. If f learned nothing, it copied the counts.

Lane (b), this work: f reads eta_in, the log count minus the log source depth d, and its output eta_out gets the log target depth d′ added back. If f learns nothing, the prediction is the source scaled by d′/d, which is the depth law.

Everything else is the old grid's: the same g, the same negative binomial noise model and loss.

Likely question: "Why let g still read depth?" PI decision: the offset should set a default, not remove capacity. Result 1 shows the price: g uses depth to move away from the law.

Methodexperiment 1 · the spread

Each design's spread now sees what its mean sees

  • Only row-1 A changed. It had one spread per pair; now it sets its spread by level, as A2 already did. Old-spread A keeps the old rule, so we can split the two changes.

The rule: whatever the mean of a design can see, its spread can see too. Panels (a) to (c) show this for A/B, C and D, which are all as in the old grid except row-1 A.

Methodidea 2 · the map h

Idea 2: h turns every possible count into a p‑value, weighted by f's prediction

  • P = −log10 p is the value the peak caller writes in every bin. h is trained against the real P track.
  • f no longer predicts p. It predicts counts; h maps them to P. Nothing is sampled.

Panel (a), left to right: f predicts a negative binomial over the count k in one bin. h reads each possible k, with the control around the bin, and gives one P per k. The predicted P is then that set of values, each weighted by its NB probability, so its mean and its CRPS are exact sums: there is no sampling.

Panel (b), the two kinds of uncertainty: push only uses only f's spread; push + own noise also gives each h(k) a learned spread of its own, for what h cannot know even with the true count.

This is an illustration with made-up shapes, not data.

Likely question: "Why should this not blow up?" There is no learned log-normal spread on P any more: the spread of P comes from the count NB, which is bounded.

Eight versions of h, from a fixed formula to a flexible network with all inputs

Rungs are input sets: rung 1 is the smallest, rung 5 is all of them (the control windows, the genome background, both depths, the control ratio, the fragment length and window, both read lengths). One h is shared by all assays; the assay is an input.

Rows go down in complexity and inputs. The two formula rows learn nothing: they are the peak caller's own Poisson tail, at the bin or over one fragment. Then affine h (a weighted sum) and MLP h (a small network) at the two input ends, rung 1 and rung 5; the design used only the two ends of a five-step input ladder (PI: "two ends only"). The last two rows add a learned correction to the formula.

Columns: each learned h comes in push only and push + own noise. Each learned h is trained two ways (the tags): on the true counts, with 3 seeds, and on f's predicted counts, with 1 seed.

The formula + correction rows need the rung-5 inputs, so they have no rung-1 version.

Likely question: "Why only one seed on f's predictions?" Compute (PI decision): the seed wobble there is borrowed from the same h trained on the true counts.

Discussionwhat we learned

What the two experiments say, as readings

Question 1 · the depth offset
The offset does not, by itself, make f follow the depth law.
Question 2 · the map h
h is stable, but in the chain it is limited by f's count errors.

Exploratory: these are readings with their seed wobble and bar, not verdicts. Results 1–6 hold the figures.

This slide answers the two questions from the motivation in plain words; it adds no new numbers, it only restates the result slides.

Question 1: the offset sets the right default, but g still reads depth and moves f away from it, so the depth law does not hold. The offset helps design A, mostly through its overall scale, and the settings other than depth still help the mean.

Question 2: with the true counts, more inputs help h most. In the chain, f's errors set the limit: training h on f's predictions does not repair them, and on some designs the chain is less accurate than the old direct prediction, but it never blows up.

Likely question: "So should we keep the offset and h?" That is a decision for after the open questions on the next slides, not a reading.

the same model trained with random settings, so it learns one average map; here it keeps d′/d.
old grid
the earlier transformation grid (t118), without the offset.
old-spread A
row-1 A with the offset but with the old grid's one spread per pair.
d and d′
the read depth of the source and of the target track, in millions of reads.
eta_in, eta_out
f's input log(1 + X) − log d and its output; the log target mean is eta_out + log d′.
depth law
on depth-only pairs, the predicted total count should scale by d′/d; we allow a 10% error.

Terms for the data and the count model, roughly in the order they appear in the deck. Point the lab here when a word on a slide is unfamiliar.

The two easiest to confuse: a design (A to D, plus a 2 for row 2) is a form of f, while a version of g (per track or across tracks) is how g is shared between tracks. Every design is trained with both versions.

Likely question: "Is the old grid the same as row 1?" No: the old grid is the whole earlier run (rows 1 and 2, without the offset); row 1 exists in both.

Appendixglossary 2 of 2 · scoring and the map h

Glossary: scoring and the map h

CRPS
a score of a whole predicted distribution against the true value; lower is better.
oracle-scaled
the part of the CRPS left after the best single rescale of the prediction.
scale error
the part of the CRPS that one overall factor would remove.
MSE of the mean
squared error of the predicted mean, on log(1 + value); it ignores the spread.
seed wobble
the largest gap between the 3 training seeds of one model (the ± on every value).
bar
2 × the larger seed wobble of two models; a gain counts only if it is larger.
blow-up
a (pair, seed) record with CRPS above 20, where typical values are below 1.
held-out chromosomes
scores are on chr19 and chr21; the best training step is picked on chr22.
map h
turns a count (and the control around it) into P; part 2's replacement for predicting p directly.
formula h
the peak caller's own Poisson tail at the local control rate; nothing learned.
affine h / MLP h
a learned weighted sum of the inputs / a small learned network.
rung 1 / rung 5
h's input sets: the bin count and the control in the bin / all inputs.
push only
the predicted P is h(k) for every count k, weighted by f's NB; h adds no noise.
push + own noise
each h(k) also gets a learned spread on log(1 + P).
chain
X → f → h: h applied to f's predicted counts, scored against the real P track.
trained on true counts / on f's NB
the two ways h is trained: on the real X′, or on f's predicted distribution.
repair
whether h trained on f's NB is lower in the chain than h trained on true counts.
merged mixture
own-noise CRPS in the chain uses at most G merged components per bin; its error is measured.
step cap / capped rows
an h fit that stopped at the maximum number of steps / an NB with more counts than the limit.

Terms for scoring and part 2. The three that matter most for reading the results are the CRPS split (oracle-scaled and scale error), the seed wobble, and the bar.

For part 2, keep apart what h is trained on (true counts or f's predictions) and what it is scored on (alone on the true counts, or in the chain on f's predictions).

Likely question: "Why two CRPS scales, on P and on log(1 + P)?" CRPS on P is comparable with the old grid; the log scale is where the own-noise h is defined and where the large values of P do not dominate.
