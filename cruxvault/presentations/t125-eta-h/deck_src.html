Motivationthe question

Can a model turn one track into another, from its processing settings alone?

  • CANDI reads these settings (we call them covariates) for every input and output track.
  • We test the settings in isolation, with small models, on data where the true X′ exists.

A track is a genome-wide signal, here in 25 bp bins. The same sample, processed with different settings (read depth, read length, control, and so on), gives different tracks. The question is whether the settings alone tell us how to go from one to the other.

The figure: on the left, a real H3K27ac track X; on the right, the same sample with fewer reads, X′. The model gets X and both sets of settings, C and C′, and must predict X′.

We need this because CANDI is conditioned on these settings, but inside CANDI we cannot see what the settings alone do.

Likely question: "Why small models?" So the settings are the only route to the answer; a big model could hide what the settings do.

Motivationthe data

We rebuilt 7 tracks many times, changing one setting at a time

Each rebuild is called an arm. We reran the ENCODE pipeline from the raw reads, so for every change we have the true answer X′. Results are always reported per mark class. The classes are a fixed table taken over from the earlier grid (H3K4me1 is listed as narrow there); they are not derived from peak widths in this work.

Panel (a): each dot is one knob we changed, with the level we moved it to. Green knobs change which reads enter the pileup, so they move both the counts and the p‑values. Purple knobs change only the control or the peak caller, so they move only the p‑values. The antibody proxy mixes control reads into the treatment, to mimic a weaker antibody.

Panel (b): the 7 base tracks fall in three mark classes with very different shapes. DNase has no control, so it has fewer arms.

Each (source arm, target arm) of one track is a pair; the model learns to go from the source to the target.

Likely question: "Why is H3K4me1 narrow?" It is the earlier grid's fixed class table, kept so the two grids compare class by class; it is a convention, not a measured property.

Motivationthe model

A small network g reads the settings and builds the transformation f

  • We train g, not f. f is only what g outputs for one pair of settings.
  • f outputs a distribution in every bin (a mean and a spread), as CANDI does: a negative binomial over counts.

Top row: g is a small neural network (2 layers of 64). It reads the source settings C and the target settings C′ and outputs the parameters θ of f.

Bottom row: f is applied to the source track X, bin by bin, and outputs a distribution for each bin of X′: a mean (the line) and a spread (the band).

Because only g sees the settings, any skill f has at turning X into X′ must come through the settings.

Likely question: "Why a distribution, not one number?" Counts are noisy; CANDI also outputs a negative binomial, and the CRPS score needs a distribution.

Motivationfour designs of f

Four designs of f, from simple to flexible

A and B look at one bin at a time, so they change magnitude only. C and D also see neighbouring bins, so they can change shape (for example, peak width).

The letters A to D name four forms of f, used everywhere in the deck. A is one straight line in log space (a slope and an intercept); B is a rising curve with 12 knots; C smooths with a kernel and then applies a curve; D is a small CNN whose layers g scales and shifts.

Read the panels left to right as "more flexible". The bottom line of each panel says what it can change and how many numbers g must output.

Likely question: "Why keep A if it is the weakest?" A is the easiest to read, and in the earlier grid it showed the clearest problem; it is also where the offset helps most (result 2).

  • Row 1: one f for the whole genome. Row 2: g also reads each bin's own level, so f can treat low bins and peaks differently. Row-2 designs carry a 2: A2, B2, C2, D2.
  • One g per track: 7 small g's. One g across tracks: a single g that also reads the assay.

Panel (b), the versions of g: either 7 separate g's, one per track, or one g for all tracks that also reads the assay.

So one "design" in the deck is a letter (A to D) plus a row (no 2 = row 1, a 2 = row 2), and every design is trained with both versions of g. A cell is one design × one version of g × one mark class.

Motivationwhat went wrong in the earlier grid, and why

The earlier grid had two problems, each with a likely cause and an idea

Panel (a): each marker is one cell of the earlier grid; the y-axis is how far the predicted total count is from the read ratio when only depth changes (the depth law), on a log scale. The green band is within 10%; almost every cell is far outside it. Likely cause: f copied the counts unless g learned to scale them.

Panel (b): how many (pair, seed) records blew up (CRPS above 20, where typical values are below 1) when the earlier grid predicted −log10 p directly. Likely cause: a learned log-normal spread σ that drifts; the log-normal mean grows as e to the σ²/2 (appendix, "the two causes in detail").

The two boxes are the two ideas tested here: a fixed depth offset in f, and computing p from predicted counts with a map h.

Likely question: "Is the depth law the right test?" It is the simplest change with a known answer: halve the reads and every bin halves on average.

Motivationthe two questions

This work asks two questions

Question 1 · the depth offset
If f scales the counts by the read ratio unless g says otherwise, do the predictions follow the depth law, and do they get closer to the truth?
Experiment 1: retrain the whole grid with the offset. Compare it with the earlier grid, and with a twin that cannot see the settings.
Question 2 · the map h
If f predicts only counts, how closely can a map h turn them into the −log10 p track, as h gets more inputs and more flexible? Does the chain f → h avoid the blow-ups?
Experiment 2: train h on the true counts and on f's predicted counts. Score it alone and in the chain f → h, against the earlier grid's p.

Exploratory: both questions get readings, with no threshold set in advance.

These are the two vault questions under the covariates question: the depth-offset question and the map-h question. Results 1 to 3 answer question 1; results 4 to 6 answer question 2.

Question 1 is about the counts only. Question 2 is about the p‑value track, first with perfect counts (how good can h be?) and then with f's predicted counts (how good is the whole chain?).

Both are exploratory by the PI's choice: the plan set no thresholds, so the deck reports readings, each with its wobble and bar.

Likely question: "Why both at once?" They share the count model: part 2 feeds the part-1 f into h.

Methodhow we score

How we score: CRPS and its two parts, the error of the mean, and a bar for noise

Lower is better for every score. All scores are on held-out chromosomes (chr19 and chr21); the best training step is picked on chr22. Each score is a mean over (pair, seed) records.

(a) CRPS scores the whole predicted distribution against the true value in each bin: a prediction that is off, or too wide, scores high. (b) We split every CRPS into an oracle-scaled part (what is left after the best single rescale) and a scale error (what that rescale removes). A gain in scale error means "the overall factor was wrong", which is cheap to fix.

(c) The MSE of the mean looks only at the predicted mean, on log(1 + value). With CRPS it tells a gain in the mean from a gain in the spread.

(d) Every model is trained with 3 seeds. The wobble is the largest gap between two seeds, and a gain counts only if it is larger than the bar, 2 × the larger wobble of the two models.

Likely question: "Why not a p‑value test?" The run is exploratory; the bar is a simple, stated rule for "larger than seed noise".

Methodidea 1 · the depth offset

Idea 1: f works on counts divided by the read depth, so "do nothing" means "scale by d′/d"

  • The only input change from the earlier grid is a constant shift per track: − log d.
  • g still reads both depths, so g can move f away from d′/d. The offset sets the default; it does not force the depth law.

Lane (a), the earlier grid: f read log(1 + X) and output the log mean of X′. If f learned nothing, it copied the counts.

Lane (b), the new model: f reads eta_in, the bin's depth-scaled level (log count minus the log source depth d), and its output eta_out gets the log target depth d′ added back. If f learns nothing, the prediction is the source scaled by d′/d, which is the depth law.

Everything else is the earlier grid's: the same g, the same negative binomial noise model and loss.

Likely question: "Why let g still read depth?" The PI chose it: the offset should set a default, not remove capacity. Whether g then uses depth to move away from the law is an open question (result 1, next steps).

Methodidea 2 and experiment 2 · the map h

Idea 2: h turns every possible count into a p‑value, weighted by f's prediction

Panel (a): f predicts a negative binomial over the count k in one bin. h reads each possible k, with the control around the bin, and gives one value P = −log10 p per k. The predicted P is that set of values, each weighted by its probability, so its mean and its CRPS are exact sums: nothing is sampled. (Shapes are an illustration, not data.)

Panel (b), two kinds of uncertainty: push only takes its spread from f only; push + own noise adds a learned spread of h's own, for what h cannot know even with the true count.

Panel (c), experiment 2: h is trained on the true counts or on f's predicted counts, and scored alone (on the true counts) or in the chain (on f's predicted counts). Three of the four cells are results 4, 5 and 6.

Likely question: "Why should this not blow up?" There is no learned log-normal spread on P any more for push-only h: the spread of P comes from the count distribution. Own-noise h do add a spread, and they can blow up (result 6).

Terms for scoring and part 2. The three that matter most for reading the results are the CRPS split (oracle-scaled and scale error), the seed wobble, and the bar.

For part 2, keep apart what h is trained on (true counts or f's predicted counts) and what it is scored on (alone on the true counts, or in the chain on f's predicted counts).

Likely question: "Why two CRPS scales, on P and on log(1 + P)?" CRPS on P is comparable with the earlier grid; the log scale is where the own-noise h is defined and where the large values of P do not dominate.

Appendixdetail of the method · the spread rule

Each design's spread now sees what its mean sees

  • The spread is the negative binomial's shape n: how wide the predicted distribution is in each bin.
  • Only row-1 A changed. It had one spread per pair; now it sets its spread by level, as A2 already did. Old-spread A keeps the old rule.

The rule: whatever the mean of a design can see, its spread can see too. Panels (a) to (c) show this for A/B, C and D, which are all as in the earlier grid except row-1 A.

Panel (d): the earlier row-1 A rule, one spread for every bin of a pair. We keep it as a separate arm, old-spread A.

Likely question: "Why change the spread at all?" One spread per pair cannot be narrow in low bins and wide in peaks; the earlier A's CRPS was dominated by that.

Appendixdetail of the motivation · the two causes

The two causes in detail

Panel (a): real data. Fewer reads scale every bin by about the read ratio (the orange line, the base times the ratio, follows the filled arm). In the earlier grid, f copied the counts unless g learned to scale them.

Panel (b): a formula, not data. The earlier grid predicted −log10 p with a log-normal whose spread σ was learned. The mean of a log-normal grows as e to the σ²/2, so a spread that drifts up makes the mean explode.

Likely question: "Why not just clip σ?" Clipping hides the drift; computing p from counts removes the learned spread from the push-only p‑value path.
